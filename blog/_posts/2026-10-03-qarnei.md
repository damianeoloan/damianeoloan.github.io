Some years ago, while learning about computer code, I completed a task involving the creation of a tribute page to a figure of one's choice. I chose [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://codepen.io/0damian/pen/XKaNzp) as one of the fathers of modern computing, and whose work encompassed a range of disciplines all of which would once have been named philosophy but are now considered more commonly as independent fields of study.

One of the more interesting elements of his work was the study of the *characteristica universalis*[^1] which underpin modern character sets, the 'emoji' in particular, which was undertaken in collaboration with Baruch Spinoza.

Leibniz was born at the outbreak of the Thirty Years War, but he appears to have been protected from its ravages and was free to travel and work in a manner not common in his age. From Leipzig, he worked in both Paris and London. His birth also coincided with the invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg in the same city.

It is not clear by what means exactly the knowledge of the Chinese pictogram character set was given to both Spinoza and Leibniz, who lived under the reign of Holy Roman Emperor, [Ferdinand III](https://grokipedia.com/page/Ferdinand_III,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), but the Roman Catholic church had already established open and formal relations with China at that point, notably under [Matteo Ricci](https://grokipedia.com/page/Matteo_Ricci) and the [Wanli Emperor](https://grokipedia.com/page/Wanli_Emperor). Their relationship features discussion of many contemporary issues, including calendars, computation, the rise of the eunuch class and the "red pill case."

Today, I chanced upon the existence of the unicode character, [U+059F](https://codepoints.net/U+059F?lang=en), which is a(n) Hebrew punctuation mark, somewhat tricky to locate, copy and paste.

> Karne parah (Hebrew: קַרְנֵי פָרָה, also spelled Qarnei Farah and other variant English spellings) is a cantillation mark found only once in the entire Torah (Book of Numbers Numbers] 35:5), and once in the Book of Esther, immediately following the identically unique Yerach ben yomo.

> The symbol of the Karne para is that of the Telisha ketana and gedola together. The melody, likewise, is that of these two more common cantillation sounds put together.

> The Hebrew words קַרְנֵי פָרָה translate into English as cow's horns.
